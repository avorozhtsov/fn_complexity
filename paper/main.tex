\begin{abstract}
We associate to a surjective map between finite sets the multiset of its
fiber cardinalities.  Cartesian products turn these signatures into a simple
asymptotic resource theory.  Its exchange rate \(C(g\mid f)\) is the largest
asymptotic number of copies of \(f\) implementable per copy of \(g\).
Power sums compute this operationally defined limit, while Gibbs
energy--entropy curves provide a geometric picture.  The comparison obtained
by putting the two directed rates side by side is not transitive: the three
signatures \(\sig{5,3}\), \(\sig{3,1,1}\), and \(\sig{6,1}\) form a strict
cycle.
\end{abstract}

\section{Signatures and implementation}

Let \(f:X\to Y\) be onto and enumerate its output as
\(Y=\{y_1,\ldots,y_r\}\).  The \emph{fiber} over \(y_i\) is the inverse image
\[
  f^{-1}(y_i)=\{x\in X:f(x)=y_i\},
  \qquad n_i=\left|f^{-1}(y_i)\right|.
\]
Thus \(n_i\) is the number of inputs mapped by \(f\) to the output \(y_i\).
After ordering these fiber sizes by decreasing value, the \emph{signature} of
\(f\) is
\[
  \sigma(f)=(n_1,\ldots,n_r),\qquad
  n_1\geq\cdots\geq n_r\geq 1,\qquad r=|Y|.
\]
Only the signature will matter below, so maps and their signatures are denoted
by the same letters.

\begin{definition}[Implemented by]
Let \(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)\) and \(b=(b_1,\ldots,b_s)\) be decreasing
signatures.  We say that \(a\) is \emph{implemented by} \(b\), and write
\(a\impl b\), when \(r\leq s\) and
\[
  a_i\leq b_i\qquad(1\leq i\leq r).
\]
\end{definition}

For maps, this means that there is an injective input relabeling
\(h_{\mathrm{in}}\) and a bijection from the used output fibers to the output
of \(f\) such that
\[
  f=h_{\mathrm{out}}\circ g\circ h_{\mathrm{in}}.
\]
More precisely, put
\[
  X'_g=h_{\mathrm{in}}(X_f),
  \qquad
  Y'_g=g(X'_g).
\]
The two inclusions on the right identify the parts of \(X_g\) and \(Y_g\)
actually used by the implementation, and the following diagram commutes:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
  X_f
    \arrow[r,"h_{\mathrm{in}}","\cong"']
    \arrow[d,"f"']
  & X'_g
    \arrow[r,hook]
    \arrow[d,"g|_{X'_g}"]
  & X_g
    \arrow[d,"g"]
  \\
  Y_f
  & Y'_g
    \arrow[l,"h_{\mathrm{out}}"',"\cong"]
    \arrow[r,hook]
  & Y_g .
\end{tikzcd}
\]
Unused fibers of \(g\) may be discarded.  The componentwise condition above
is exactly the condition that every fiber of \(f\) can be injected into its
assigned fiber of \(g\).

The Cartesian product \(a\otimes b\) has all products \(a_i b_j\) as its
fiber sizes, with multiplicity.  We write \(a^{\otimes k}\) for the \(k\)-fold
product.

\begin{definition}[Exchange rate]
For signatures \(f\) and \(g\), set
\[
 k_{\max}^{g\leftarrow f}(n)
 =\max\left\{k\geq0:f^{\otimes k}\impl g^{\otimes n}\right\}.
\]
The exchange rate, with the implementer written first, is
\[
  C(g\mid f)
  =\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{k_{\max}^{g\leftarrow f}(n)}{n}.
\]
\end{definition}

The sequence \(k_{\max}\) is superadditive: implementations for \(n\) and
\(m\) copies can be tensored.  Fekete's lemma therefore gives the limit
(possibly \(+\infty\)) and identifies it with the corresponding supremum.
Thus the limit is the definition; the infimum below is a computational
characterization.

\section{Power sums and Gibbs curves}

For a signature \(a=(a_i)\), define its power-sum partition function
\[
  Z_a(\beta)=\sum_i a_i^\beta,\qquad \beta\geq0,
\]
where \(\beta=1/T\) is inverse physical temperature.  Throughout this paper,
\(T\) denotes temperature and never inverse temperature.
The fibers of the \(k\)-fold Cartesian power \(a^{\otimes k}\) are indexed by
\((i_1,\ldots,i_k)\) and have sizes \(a_{i_1}\cdots a_{i_k}\).  Consequently,
\begin{align}
  Z_{a^{\otimes k}}(\beta)
  &=\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_k}
    (a_{i_1}\cdots a_{i_k})^\beta \notag\\
  &=\left(\sum_i a_i^\beta\right)^k
   =Z_a(\beta)^k,
  \qquad
  \log Z_{a^{\otimes k}}(\beta)=k\log Z_a(\beta).
  \label{eq:tensor-partition}
\end{align}
If \(f^{\otimes k}\impl g^{\otimes n}\), applying the power sum at every
\(\beta\geq0\) gives
\[
  Z_f(\beta)^k\leq Z_g(\beta)^n,
  \qquad
  \frac{k}{n}\leq
  \frac{\log Z_g(\beta)}{\log Z_f(\beta)}.
\]
Thus Cartesian products become ordinary scalar multiplication after taking
\(\log Z\).  Passing to the asymptotic optimum gives the upper bound in the
following formula.

The asymptotic comparison is governed by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rate}
  C(g\mid f)
  =\inf_{\beta\geq0}
    \frac{\log Z_g(\beta)}{\log Z_f(\beta)},
\end{equation}
together with the endpoint
\[
  \lim_{\beta\to\infty}
  \frac{\log Z_g(\beta)}{\log Z_f(\beta)}
  =\frac{\log\max_j g_j}{\log\max_i f_i}.
\]
Necessity follows by applying every power sum to an implementation.
Asymptotic sufficiency follows by sorting the product fibers and applying the
usual exponential, or large-deviation, estimates to their ranks.  In this
way all componentwise constraints are represented by the one-parameter
family \(Z_a(\beta)\).

Put
\[
  \epsilon_i=-\log a_i,\qquad
  p_i(T)
  =\frac{e^{-\epsilon_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{-\epsilon_j/T}}
  =\frac{a_i^{1/T}}{Z_a(1/T)}.
\]
These are the Gibbs probabilities at physical temperature \(T\).  Their mean
energy and entropy are
\[
  E_a(T)=\sum_i p_i(T)\epsilon_i,\qquad
  H_a(T)=-\sum_i p_i(T)\log p_i(T).
\]
Writing \(\beta=1/T\), they satisfy
\(\log Z_a(\beta)=H_a(T)-E_a(T)/T\).  Moreover,
\[
 H_{a^{\otimes k}}(T)=kH_a(T),\qquad
 E_{a^{\otimes k}}(T)=kE_a(T),
\]
because the Gibbs distribution of the Cartesian power is the product
distribution.  Eliminating \(T\) gives
\[
 E_{a^{\otimes k}}(H)=kE_a(H/k),
\]
so taking Cartesian powers is a homothety by \(k\) in the
\((E,H)\)-plane.

Geometrically,
\(\log Z_a(\beta)=H_a(T)-\beta E_a(T)\) is the offset of the supporting
line with normal \((-\beta,1)\) to the convex Gibbs region \(\mathcal R_a\).
Therefore formula \eqref{eq:rate} is equivalently
\[
 C(g\mid f)
 =\sup\{c\geq0:c\,\mathcal R_f\subseteq\mathcal R_g\}.
\]
In particular,
\(C(g\mid f)\Gamma_f\) fits in the convex Gibbs region bounded by
\(\Gamma_g\).  Equivalently, the tight homothetic copy of \(g\) embracing
\(f\) is \(C(g\mid f)^{-1}\Gamma_g\).

\section{The pair
\texorpdfstring{\(\sig{2,2}\) and \(\sig{3,1}\)}
{{2,2} and {3,1}}}

Let \(g=\sig{2,2}\) and \(f=\sig{3,1}\).  Every fiber of
\(g^{\otimes n}\) has size \(2^n\).  The largest fiber of
\(f^{\otimes k}\) has size \(3^k\), and the remaining rank constraint is
weaker at the optimum.  Hence
\[
  C(\sig{2,2}\mid\sig{3,1})
  =\frac{\log2}{\log3}
  =0.6309297536\ldots.
\]
The reverse direction is limited at a finite temperature:
\begin{align*}
  C(\sig{3,1}\mid\sig{2,2})
  &=\min_{\beta\geq0}
    \frac{\log(3^\beta+1)}{(\beta+1)\log2} \\
  &=0.9653844417\ldots,
  \qquad
  \beta_*=0.4036799030\ldots,\quad
  T_*=\beta_*^{-1}=2.47721027\ldots.
\end{align*}
The large asymmetry is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:pair}.  Solid lines are the
original Gibbs boundaries.  In each panel the other signature is enlarged by
the reciprocal directed rate; only this resulting tight boundary is dotted.
The thin horizontal line joins the \(T=\infty\) endpoint to \(E=0\).  When the
\(T=0\) endpoint has residual entropy \(H(0)>0\), a thin vertical line joins
that endpoint to \(H=0\); no vertical line is needed when \(H(0)=0\).
Endpoint closures of homothetically scaled boundaries are thin and dotted,
matching the scaled curve; endpoint closures of original boundaries are thin
and solid.  The \(85\%\)-transparent fills extend horizontally from each
boundary to \(E=0\), displaying the closed convex Gibbs regions.  Since
\(\sig{2,2}\) has two equal energy levels, its canonical Gibbs boundary
itself collapses to the single point
\((E,H)=(-\log2,\log2)\).

\section{A two-sided finite-temperature tangency}

Finite-temperature contact can determine both directed rates.  Consider
\[
  a=\sig{6,5,2,1},
  \qquad
  b=\sig{6,4,3,2}.
\]
Because \(a\) and \(b\) have the same length and the same largest fiber,
both endpoint comparisons are equal to one:
\[
 \frac{\log Z_a(0)}{\log Z_b(0)}=1,
 \qquad
 \lim_{\beta\to\infty}
 \frac{\log Z_a(\beta)}{\log Z_b(\beta)}
 =\frac{\log6}{\log6}=1.
\]
Nevertheless, the two directed minima are strictly smaller than one and occur
at finite positive temperatures:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Direction & \(C\) & \(T_*\) & \(\beta_*=1/T_*\) \\
\midrule
\(C(a\mid b)\) &
\(0.9664896992\) & \(1.7970865612\) & \(0.5564562229\) \\
\(C(b\mid a)\) &
\(0.9786567233\) & \(0.2459449336\) & \(4.0659508025\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At an interior minimizer of
\(R(\beta)=\log Z_g(\beta)/\log Z_f(\beta)\), the equation
\(R'(\beta_*)=0\), together with \(\log Z=H-\beta E\), gives
\[
  E_g(T_*)=C(g\mid f)E_f(T_*),
  \qquad
  H_g(T_*)=C(g\mid f)H_f(T_*).
\]
Thus the scaled Gibbs boundary and the target boundary meet at the same point.
Moreover
\[
  \frac{dH}{dE}=\frac{1}{T_*}=\beta_*,
\]
which is invariant under homothety, so their tangent lines also agree.  Both
contacts in Figure~\ref{fig:finite-temperature} are therefore genuine
finite-temperature tangencies rather than endpoint contacts.

  by \(1/C(a\mid b)\) to embrace \(b\).  Crosses mark the interior contact
  points.  Thin dotted horizontal lines close the scaled regions at their
  \(T=\infty\) endpoints; translucent fills extend to \(E=0\).}

\section{A cyclic strict comparison}

\begin{definition}
For signatures \(a,b\), define
\[
  a\prec b
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  C(a\mid b)<C(b\mid a).
\]
\end{definition}

This is a natural pairwise comparison: \(b\) is declared more complex when
it converts into \(a\) more efficiently than \(a\) converts into \(b\).
Nevertheless, it is not a strict linear order.

Set
\[
  f_1=\sig{5,3},\qquad
  f_2=\sig{3,1,1},\qquad
  f_3=\sig{6,1}.
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:rate} gives the comparisons in
Table~\ref{tab:cycle}.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \caption{Directed rates producing a strict cycle.}
  \label{tab:cycle}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}cccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Pair & Forward rate & Reverse rate & Conclusion \\
    \midrule
    \(f_1,f_2\) & \(C(f_1\mid f_2)=0.630929754\)
                & \(C(f_2\mid f_1)=0.670211928\) & \(f_1\prec f_2\) \\
    \(f_2,f_3\) & \(C(f_2\mid f_3)=0.613147193\)
                & \(C(f_3\mid f_2)=0.630929754\) & \(f_2\prec f_3\) \\
    \(f_3,f_1\) & \(C(f_3\mid f_1)=0.897931851\)
                & \(C(f_1\mid f_3)=0.898244402\) & \(f_3\prec f_1\) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Thus
\[
  f_1\prec f_2,\qquad f_2\prec f_3,\qquad f_3\prec f_1.
\]
A strict linear order must be transitive, so this three-cycle proves that
\(\prec\) is not a linear order.  Figure~\ref{fig:cycle} shows all three
pairwise comparisons geometrically.  In the panel targeting \(f_i\), the two
dotted curves are \(C(f_j\mid f_i)^{-1}\Gamma_{f_j}\); each is the tight
homothetic boundary embracing \(\Gamma_{f_i}\).

\section{A thermodynamic analogy and its limits}

There is an appealing physical reading of the construction.  Regard a
signature \(A=(a_i)\) as one idealized particle whose internal energy levels
are \(\epsilon_i=-\log a_i\).  The Cartesian power \(A^{\otimes k}\) is then a
system of \(k\) independent particles of type \(A\).  Likewise, a second
signature \(B\) represents a second particle type.  At an interior contact of
their homothetically scaled Gibbs boundaries,
\[
  \frac{dH_A}{dE}=\frac{dH_B}{dE}=\frac{1}{T},
\]
so the active comparison has a common temperature.  This makes the
finite-temperature tangencies above analogous to reversible contact between
two gases of particles.

The analogy must not be read as a law of spontaneous reaction.  The relation
\(\impl\) is a combinatorial possibility, not a microscopic dynamics, and
\(C\) is a conversion capacity rather than a chemical affinity.  For a
putative conversion
\[
  kA\rightleftharpoons nB
\]
put \(r=k/n\).  The definitions give the two distinct conditions
\begin{align}
  B^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow A^{\otimes k}
  &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  r\leq C(B\mid A)
  &&\text{in the asymptotic closure}, \label{eq:B-to-A}\\
  A^{\otimes k}\longrightarrow B^{\otimes n}
  &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  r\geq \frac{1}{C(A\mid B)}
  &&\text{in the asymptotic closure}. \label{eq:A-to-B}
\end{align}
Strict inequalities guarantee the corresponding direction for sufficiently
large block sizes; an exact boundary ratio need not be attained at finite
size.
Thus a single inequality involving \(C(A\mid B)\) does not choose the
direction.  In fact,
\[
  C(A\mid B)C(B\mid A)\leq1,
\]
so the two thresholds form a conversion window
\[
  C(B\mid A)\leq r\leq\frac{1}{C(A\mid B)}.
\]
Away from equality there can be ratios in this window for which neither
direction realizes that exact stoichiometry.  The width of the window is an
irreversibility loss, analogous to a bid--ask spread.  When the endpoints
coincide, the comparison is asymptotically reversible.  An interior tangency
identifies the Gibbs temperature at which the corresponding supporting
constraint is tight.

For comparison, genuine chemical equilibrium at fixed temperature and
pressure would require zero reaction affinity,
\[
  \mathcal A=k\mu_A-n\mu_B=0,
\]
in addition to equal temperature.  Here \(\mu_A,\mu_B\) are chemical
potentials.  This condition is not part of the definition of
implementation.  Consequently, the rate inequalities should be called
\emph{allowed conversion directions}, not spontaneous reaction directions.

A constant reaction-energy term can nevertheless be represented cleanly.
For
\[
  kA\rightleftharpoons nB+q
\]
shift every level of \(B\) by \(q/n\):
\[
  \widetilde\epsilon_{B,j}=\epsilon_{B,j}+\frac qn.
\]
Then
\[
  \widetilde E_B(H)=E_B(H)+\frac qn,\qquad
  \widetilde Z_B(T)=e^{-q/(nT)}Z_B(T),
\]
while \(H_B(T)\) and the tangent slope \(dH_B/dE=1/T\) are unchanged.  Hence
the \(n\)-particle energy region moves horizontally by \(q\).  This bookkeeping
is valid when \(q\) is a fixed energy offset carried by an explicit work or
storage degree of freedom.  Heat passed to a bath also carries entropy and
cannot, in general, be modeled by a horizontal shift alone.

Several physical effects are deliberately absent: interactions, quantum
symmetrization, translational and volume degrees of freedom, mixing entropy
between particle types, conservation laws, reaction kinetics, activation
barriers, and coupling to a heat or particle reservoir.  The logarithmic
energies are dimensionless (\(k_B=1\)), and relative energy offsets between
particle types must be fixed because a conversion may change particle
number.  Subject to these simplifications, the construction suggests a
broader question: use homothetic containment of Gibbs regions to compare the
complexity of general Gibbs systems.

The supporting function
\[
  \Psi_A(T)=\log Z_A(T)=-\frac{F_A(T)}{T}
\]
is the Massieu potential, or free entropy.  Thus
\[
  C(A\mid B)=\inf_{T>0}\frac{\Psi_A(T)}{\Psi_B(T)}
\]
is the largest factor \(c\) for which
\(\Psi_A(T)\geq c\Psi_B(T)\) simultaneously at every temperature.  It may be
read as an all-temperature, or worst-temperature, conversion rate; an
interior minimizing \(T_*\) is the bottleneck temperature.  The closest
established framework is the resource theory without a fixed background
temperature developed by
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.01302}
{Sparaciari, Oppenheim, and Fritz}.  There, asymptotically many
noninteracting systems are classified by energy and entropy, and conversion
rates are read from a two-dimensional energy--entropy diagram.  Our
construction specializes this idea to complete Gibbs boundaries and requires
the comparison at all temperatures rather than relative to one prescribed
heat bath.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/relativistic-radiation-energy-entropy.pdf}
  \caption{A real exact homothety in the ultrarelativistic limit.  At the same
  temperature, the electron--positron sector has \(7/4\) times the energy and
  entropy densities of the photon sector.  The dotted homothetic photon curve
  coincides with the electron--positron curve.}
  \label{fig:radiation-sectors}
\end{figure}

A simple real system realizes the homothety exactly.  For an
ultrarelativistic equilibrium sector \(X\) with effective number \(g_X\) of
internal degrees of freedom, in natural units and fixed volume,
\[
  E_X(T)=\frac{\pi^2}{30}g_XVT^4,\qquad
  H_X(T)=\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_XVT^3.
\]
Consequently, two such sectors \(A,B\) obey
\[
  E_A(T)=\frac{g_A}{g_B}E_B(T),\qquad
  H_A(T)=\frac{g_A}{g_B}H_B(T)
\]
at every temperature: their entire energy--entropy boundaries are
homothetic, with \(C=g_A/g_B\).  Photons have \(g_\gamma=2\), whereas an
ultrarelativistic electron--positron sector has the fermionic effective value
\(g_{e^\pm}=(7/8)\times4=7/2\).  Hence its factor relative to photons is
\(7/4\), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:radiation-sectors}.  The early-universe
reaction \(e^-+e^+\rightleftharpoons2\gamma\) maintained thermal and chemical
equilibrium in the relativistic regime; when the temperature fell below the
electron mass, annihilation transferred entropy to the photon bath.  Detailed
calculations confirm that most annihilation occurred near equilibrium and
that the process affects big-bang nucleosynthesis and the effective
relativistic degrees of freedom
\href{https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.101.063507}{[Thomas et al., 2020]}.

For arbitrary Gibbs systems this should initially be viewed as a proposed
complexity comparison: unlike finite-map signatures, its operational
interpretation and the treatment of infinite spectra require separate
analysis.

\section{Conclusion}

Fiber signatures retain only a very small part of a finite map, yet their
Cartesian products already produce a nontrivial asymptotic resource theory.
The operational rate \(C(g\mid f)\), the power-sum formula, and the
energy--entropy homotheties are three views of the same comparison.  The
example above also warns against compressing the two directed rates into a
single global ranking: pairwise exchange advantage can be cyclic.
The thermodynamic analogy suggests using the same Gibbs-region comparison
for broader systems while keeping conversion capacity distinct from physical
reaction affinity.

\section{Unrestricted input and output maps}

Suppose that the maps \(h_{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(h_{\mathrm{out}}\) in the
definition of implementation are allowed to be arbitrary.  Then \(b\) can be
implemented by \(a\) exactly when
\[
  |Y_b|\leq |Y_a|.
\]
Indeed, arbitrary \(h_{\mathrm{in}}\) may send every element of a fiber of
\(b\) to one chosen input of \(a\), so the fiber sizes impose no constraint.
Conversely, a deterministic \(h_{\mathrm{out}}\) cannot produce more distinct
outputs than \(a\) has.

For Cartesian powers the condition is therefore
\[
  |Y_b|^k\leq |Y_a|^n,
  \qquad
  k_{\max}(n)
  =\left\lfloor
    n\frac{\log|Y_a|}{\log|Y_b|}
   \right\rfloor.
\]
Consequently, when \(|Y_a|,|Y_b|>1\),
\[
  \boxed{\displaystyle
  C(a\mid b)=\frac{\log|Y_a|}{\log|Y_b|}}.
\]
Thus \(C(a\mid b)C(b\mid a)=1\), and the comparison from
Definition~3 reduces to
\[
  a\prec b
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  |Y_a|<|Y_b|.
\]
It is a total preorder on signatures, with equal-size output sets tied, and
becomes a linear order after identifying signatures having the same
\(|Y|\).  In partition-function language only the endpoint
\(Z_a(0)=|Y_a|\) remains; the fiber sizes and the rest of the Gibbs curve play
no role.  If \(|Y_b|=1\), then \(C(a\mid b)=+\infty\); if
\(|Y_a|=1<|Y_b|\), then \(C(a\mid b)=0\).

\section{The first 99 non-special signatures}

Singleton signatures \(\sig{N}\) and all-ones signatures
\(\sig{1,\ldots,1}\) form exceptional infinite families and are omitted from
this finite list.  Starting from the catalog used for the exchange matrix, we
adjoin all length-two signatures with largest entry \(7\) or \(8\), contract
the strongly connected components of the strict comparison graph, and use
the deterministic topological order described in the analysis code.

Because \(\prec\) is not transitive, the resulting numbered list is not a
linear extension of every pairwise comparison.  In
Table~\ref{tab:signature-order-exceptions}, a row for \(x\) records every
earlier numbered signature \(a\) for which \(x\prec a\), together with the two
directed rates.  Thus each listed entry satisfies
\[
  C(x\mid a)<C(a\mid x)
\]
despite \(a\) appearing before \(x\).
